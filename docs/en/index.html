Why track this line, not model capability

Almost every public conversation circles one question: when will models outperform human analysts? It is the wrong question. Finance has never been short of clever judgment. What it lacks is the authority to turn judgment into action.

In 2024 the Bank of England and the FCA surveyed 118 firms. The number everyone quotes is that 75% already use AI. The same survey holds three numbers almost nobody mentions: foundation models make up only 17% of use cases; 55% of use cases involve some automated decision-making, yet only 2% are fully autonomous; and firms rate 62% of their use cases as low materiality.

Put together, they point the opposite way from "full embrace": AI has spread through the periphery of finance but has not entered its core. It writes copy, answers customers and helps engineers code. It does not approve loans, place orders, move funds or sign filings.

That isn't because the models aren't smart enough. The real obstacle is elsewhere: nobody is willing to answer for a system that can't be explained, traced or made to pay for its mistakes.

How much money is an AI system allowed to move without a human reviewing each transaction?

This line is worth tracking because it is verifiable — tenders, contract clauses and incident disclosures will spell it out; it shapes revenue models — systems that can move money are priced on outcomes, systems that can't are priced per seat, an order of magnitude apart; and it is non-linear — each step needs governance, liability and insurance to arrive together, so it stalls for years and then jumps.

The core framework: automation depth L0–L4

Levels are defined not by model capability but by three observable facts: can the system reach conclusions alone, can it act alone, and who bears the consequences.

L0
Observe Reads and organizes; produces no business conclusions. Trusted amount: 0 Widespread
L1
Advise Produces conclusions, but a human accepts each one. ROI can't be attributed, so it's the hardest product to renew. Mainstream form, 2023 — 2025
L2
Bounded execution Acts automatically within limits, allowlists and scenario boundaries; humans spot-check afterwards. This is the rung where zero breaks. In production 2026 — 2028 · today's frontier
L3
Conditional autonomy Executes by default and escalates only exceptions; the institution holds reserves or insurance against outcomes. First seen 2029 — 2033
L4
Full autonomy No preset limits; systems negotiate and execute across institutions. Not mainstream before 2035
Why steps, not a ramp: liability, insurance and audit are all discrete. The law doesn't accept "the system is 70% responsible," there's no actuarial pricing without incident data, and auditors can't issue a "partially compliant" opinion. Every step needs an explicit liability shift. So what's worth watching isn't model launches but the wording of liability clauses.

Four control rights

The cost of access breaks down into four rights that can be held — and taken away — separately. The combination decides whether a company collects rent or merely takes orders.

RightWhat it isHow to tell you really hold it
DataOutcome labels that keep flowing back, not static purchased corporaWould the data go stale the moment you stopped operating?
EntryThe authoritative home of a class of business factsHow many years of history and configuration would a customer have to migrate to replace you?
ExecutionInitiating real money movement or filings without a third partyCan your conclusion become an action without going through someone else's system?
LiabilityPresenting complete evidence and absorbing losses when things go wrongCan you reconstruct the decision chain for a regulator and say who pays?

Model progress lowers the cost of judgment, not the cost of access. Access is built from licences, clearing networks, dispute processes and liability capacity, and it rises rather than falls as regulation tightens. The two blades of that scissors gap point in the direction value is moving.

Six bets

Each has a time frame and a falsification condition. If a falsification condition appears first, the right response is to overturn the bet, not to explain it away.

  1. The governance stack becomes a procurement threshold before model capability does Evaluation, logging, permissions, fallback and exit enter tenders as standalone clauses; vendors that fall short can't bid. 2026 — 2028 · High confidence
  2. The trusted amount clearly leaves zero in several areas In at least one of refunds, reconciliation, collections or fraud prevention, execution without per-transaction review appears, with limits and rollback. 2026 — 2029 · High confidence
  3. Mandatory incident disclosure arrives before insurance products Regulators require AI production incidents to be reported and failure modes shared; only then does insurable AI-decision liability cover appear. 2027 — 2031 · Medium confidence
  4. Agent payments will run on competing standards for a long time Authorization semantics and chargeback liability won't unify within a decade; cross-ecosystem recognition stays bilateral. 2027 — 2035 · Medium confidence
  5. Value splits three ways: execution, facts and governance No single AI operating system for finance emerges; the governance layer forms last and is the most underrated. 2028 — 2035 · Medium confidence
  6. Fully autonomous finance won't be mainstream before 2035 Unreviewed lending, advice and trade execution will keep limits and scope boundaries for a long time. 2030 — 2035 · High confidence

Method and limits

First establish what has already happened from regulatory documents, audited annual reports and international-organization reports; then build conditional projections on those facts, each with observable triggers and falsification conditions. Claims that can't be falsified are, as far as possible, left out.

Data verification: every public figure was checked against its primary source on 11 September 2026 (original regulatory surveys and legislation, company financials and results announcements, official product pages). Nine statements were corrected before the first edition was finalized, three of them substantive errors; the corrections are preserved in the repository's commit history.

That doesn't make the book error-free: company-reported figures can't be independently verified and are used only to judge direction; product-page figures change over time and reflect the access date; the author is not a finance professional and may misread regulatory documents; anything marked "research judgment" is inference, not fact. If you find an error, please raise it in the repository; corrections go into the version history.
Evidence-discount rules: company-reported efficiency gains indicate direction only; unaudited revenue and user numbers never enter structural conclusions; media valuations and funding rumors are not used; product launches don't count as commercial proof; "institutions covered" does not equal paid penetration.

Three known blind spots: insurance is barely discussed; a gradual geopolitical environment is assumed; the perspective is mainly institutional, with consumer acceptance treated as exogenous.

Nothing here is investment advice. All company facts come from public disclosures; no non-public information is used.

What this book argues

Almost every public conversation about AI in finance circles one question: when will models outperform human analysts? It is the wrong question. Finance has never been short of clever judgment. What it lacks is the authority to turn judgment into action.

So this book tracks a single line: how much money an AI system is allowed to move without a human reviewing it. I call it the trusted-amount ceiling. At most institutions today it is zero. But it is leaving zero, and it moves in steps: each step is unlocked by a specific, observable set of conditions.

This line is more worth watching than any capability curve. It is verifiable — tender documents and incident disclosures will spell it out. It shapes revenue models — systems that can move money are priced on outcomes, systems that cannot are priced per seat. And it is non-linear: unlocking each step needs governance, liability and insurance to fall into place together, so the line stalls for years and then jumps.

The book has five chapters: establish the trend line, project the end state, take apart four fronts of competition, examine how regulators enter, and close with six falsifiable bets and a tracking list.

PREFACEA Number That Has Always Been Zero

A number that has always been zero

In 2024 the Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority ran an industry-wide survey: 75% of responding financial firms were already using AI, and another 10% planned to within three years. The figure travelled widely, usually as proof that finance has "fully embraced AI."[4]

The same survey contains three other numbers that almost nobody quotes. First, foundation models account for only about 17% of use cases — three-quarters of "AI applications" are still the predictive models of a decade ago. Second, 55% of use cases involve some degree of automated decision-making, but only 2% are fully autonomous. Third, firms rate 62% of their use cases as low materiality and only 16% as high.[4]

Put the three together and the conclusion is the opposite of "full embrace": AI has spread through the periphery of finance but has not entered its core. It writes marketing copy, answers customer questions and helps engineers code. It does not approve loans, place orders, move funds or sign tax filings.

This is not because the models aren't smart enough. In many regulated activities, model judgment already beats a junior employee. The real obstacle lies elsewhere: nobody is willing to answer for a system that can't be explained, traced or made to pay for its mistakes.

So the variable worth tracking is not capability but authority. Specifically, this number:

2%Fully autonomous use casesAnother 24% are semi-autonomous: they make some decisions alone, but critical or ambiguous calls are designed to go to a human.[4]

How much money is an AI system allowed to move without a human reviewing each transaction?

At the vast majority of financial institutions today, the answer is zero. Not "small" — zero. Any action that touches funds, credit or a regulatory filing needs a natural person to sign off somewhere, even when the signature is a formality.

Zero is a peculiar number. It means the curve hasn't started, so nobody models it and nobody prices it. The market still values AI-in-finance companies on "how much labor they save" — pricing third-generation products as if they were second-generation ones.

But zero is breaking. Stripe says its adaptive acceptance recovered a record of roughly $6 billion in falsely declined legitimate transactions in 2024, up 60% year on year, and Radar claims an average 32% reduction in fraud for its users.[16][17][18] Intuit reports that more than 3 million customers have let AI agents do work for them, with historical repeat usage above 85%.[19][20] Ant Group has launched a payments MCP server and an AI wallet, plainly aimed at plugging agents into money movement.[14][24]

These are all still small-value, high-frequency, reversible actions. What they share is this: the system made a decision without anyone looking at the individual case, and that decision directly changed where money went. Zero is no longer zero.

Why "zero" matters so muchIn self-driving the equivalent variable is hands-off time; in industrial automation, unattended run time. In finance it is the trusted amount. Every curve of this kind hugs the floor for years, then climbs in steps.
Shifts already under wayAutomatic recovery of false declines, automatic matching of bank feeds, agent payment tokens — in all three the system changes where money flows, just in small, reversible amounts.

The three examples come from very different companies: a payments network, a tax-and-accounting software firm and a super-app. They share no model vendor and no jurisdiction. What they do share is an execution interface. Capability spreads everywhere; authority does not. Authority flows only to those who already hold the interface and the capacity to bear liability.

What the three have in commonNone leads on models; all hold execution interfaces. This is the book's core observation.

PREFACENobody is pricing this in

Nobody is pricing this in

The World Economic Forum estimates that AI spending in financial services will grow from about $35 billion in 2023 to about $97 billion in 2027.[5] The figure is often used to argue the market is large. But if you believe the trusted amount will leave zero, the forecast is badly understated — because it counts money spent on AI, not money that passes through AI.

The two differ by orders of magnitude. One payments company processes $1.9 trillion a year.[16] If even one ten-thousandth of those decisions shifted from people to systems, the commercial value would not be a software licence fee but a take rate on that flow. Software budgets and transaction take rates sit three to four orders of magnitude apart.

That is what I mean by "nobody is pricing it in." Today's market is valuing a software market. If this trend line holds, what actually forms is an infrastructure market — one that, like clearing houses, card networks and exchanges, charges by volume rather than by seat.

Three premises

Premise one: model capability will keep commoditizing. I do not assume any company achieves a durable lead in models. On the contrary, the whole argument rests on models becoming interchangeable inputs. If that is wrong, the book's conclusions weaken considerably.

Premise two: regulation tightens rather than loosens. China's 32 guiding opinions of June 2026, the US Treasury's FS AI RMF of February 2026 (with 230 control objectives), and the EU's AI Act and DORA all point the same way: risk-tiered, full-lifecycle, traceable.[10][11][12][13] I assume that direction holds.

Premise three: liability does not disappear. However the technology changes, finance must ultimately answer "who pays when it goes wrong?" Any product design that sidesteps that question will be stopped at the door of the core process.

CHAPTER 1 · THE FIVE-RUNG LADDER1.1 Making "trusted" countable

1.1  Making "trusted" countable

Self-driving levels are useful not because they describe the technology precisely, but because they translate a continuous technical problem into a discrete liability problem. The real difference between L2 and L3 isn't how well the car drives; it is who is responsible in a crash — the driver at L2, the system provider at L3. The industry was stuck between those two levels for years, and perception algorithms were never what held it back.

Finance needs the same kind of scale, and has better reason to: every action in finance already carries a clear owner and audit requirements. Yet no widely accepted framework exists, so the debate stays stuck on unfalsifiable questions like "can AI be a financial adviser?"

I propose a five-level framework. It is defined not by model capability but by three things: whether the system can reach conclusions on its own, whether it can act on its own, and who bears the consequences when it does.

L0 Observe. The system only reads and organizes; it produces no business conclusions. Report generation, data retrieval and document classification live here. Most first-year "AI transformation" results stop at this level.

L1 Advise. The system produces conclusions, but a human must accept each one. This was the standard shape of generative copilots from 2023 to 2025. Its commercial problem isn't that it doesn't work — it's that value can't be attributed. A person still makes the final call, so you can't prove the improvement came from AI.

L2 Bounded execution. The system acts automatically within preset limits, allowlists and scenario boundaries; humans spot-check afterwards. This is the rung where zero breaks: from here on, the system's output directly changes where money goes. Today's most advanced products are early in this level.

L3 Conditional autonomy. The system executes by default and escalates only exceptions. The institution formally accepts that routine decisions belong to the system and holds reserves or insurance against them. This level has not yet appeared widely in regulated core financial processes.

L4 Full autonomy. No preset limits; systems negotiate and execute across institutions. It won't be mainstream within the foreseeable decade, but it shapes the end state, so it earns its own discussion (Chapter 2).

How levels are definedNot by how strong the model is, but by three observable facts: can it reach conclusions alone, can it act alone, and who bears the consequences. All three leave evidence in contracts and incident disclosures.
Why not an "automation rate"An automation rate lumps L0 report generation together with L2 automatic loan disbursement. Their commercial and risk profiles differ by orders of magnitude; a metric that blends them means nothing.
The L1 commercial trapCopilots are the easiest products to build and the hardest to renew. A human makes the final decision, so ROI can't be attributed, and you negotiate every renewal from weakness. This is a structural flaw of second-generation products, not an execution problem.
L2 is the watershedIt is the first level at which outcome-based pricing makes logical sense — the system's action now has a direct causal link to the business result.

CHAPTER 1 · THE FIVE-RUNG LADDER1.2 What unlocks each rung

1.2  What unlocks each rung

On technology alone, getting from L1 to L2 needs almost nothing new: wire the model's output to an API and you're done. Plenty of teams can demo it in a week. But what separates the demo from production isn't a technical gap. It is three sets of prerequisites, all missing at once.

Technical prerequisites are usually met first, and are the easiest to mistake for the only ones: confidence can be estimated, actions can be rolled back, and everything leaves a trail. None of this is hard; many teams simply haven't done it, because a demo doesn't need it.

Governance prerequisites are the real bottleneck. They require a jointly agreed evaluation set with a human baseline, tiered authorization, circuit breakers and fallback paths, a complete chain of audit events, and proof that the vendor can be replaced. Not one of these can be solved by model capability. They are all problems of product and organizational design.

Commercial prerequisites are the most often overlooked: there must be a buyer willing to pay for outcomes and empowered to transfer some of the liability. When the buyer is the IT department, procurement inevitably falls back on "how many heads did it save." Only when the buyer owns the business line is there pricing room for products above L2.

A demo in a week, not in production after two yearsThis is the shared experience of almost every AI project in finance. A demo needs only the technical prerequisites; production needs all three sets at once.
Governance isn't compliance's jobEvaluation sets, tiered authorization, circuit breakers and audit events are all product features. Leaving them for compliance to "add later" pushes launch back a year.

This leads to a corollary that isn't kind to startups: of the three prerequisites, only the technical one can be achieved by a team on its own. The other two must be built together with the buyer. Products above L2 cannot be made in a lab.

That is what makes a "design partner" special. What you need isn't feature feedback but an organization willing to share your first incident with you.

The key corollaryThe next winners won't be the companies with the best models but the ones that assemble all three prerequisites first. This frames the analysis in every chapter that follows.
Seen from the other sideIf you are building a pure model or pure application-layer product, this table is bad news: your technical edge is depreciating, and the two columns you lack can't be closed by working weekends.

1.3  Where we stand

Place six major areas of finance on this framework and the picture is strikingly uneven. It says more about where the industry really is than any overall adoption rate.

Only one area runs steadily at L2: payment risk. It approves or blocks transactions automatically; humans only spot-check afterwards and handle disputes. The reason is easy to see — the area meets all three prerequisites by nature: outcome labels flow back in real time, actions are reversible, a dispute process already exists, and the buyer is the business itself.

Customer service and operations are moving from L1 to L2. The boundary is "may change state but not move money": the system can update an address, cancel an order or reship, but refunds are still capped.

Tax, accounting and reconciliation is the next most likely area to cross into L2: answers are objectively right or wrong, systems of record exist, and the industry has a tradition of expert backstops.[19][20]

Research and wealth management sits at L1 and won't move soon. The obstacle isn't technology but suitability obligations and mis-selling liability, which make "unreviewed advice" hard to sustain in law.

Credit and pricing is also stuck at L1: explainability and fairness reviews require every feature to be justified, which rules out a large class of end-to-end approaches.

Trading and treasury is the most conservative cell. It has sandboxes, limits and layered reviews, and its tail risk dwarfs the others — a failure doesn't lose one order; it can set off a market-wide chain reaction.[7]

Look at the six together and a clear rule emerges: how far an area can go depends on how quickly its errors are discovered, and whether they can be undone once they are. A payment-risk error surfaces in seconds and can be reversed in minutes; bad investment advice may take months to show and can't be undone. This rule explains today's distribution better than "high risk versus low risk."

It also gives a practical filter. When assessing any AI opportunity in finance, ask three questions first: How quickly is an error discovered? Can it be undone? Who bears the cost of undoing it? If all three answers are clear, the area has a shot at L2. If any one is unclear, it will stay at the advice layer.

Note that the filter has nothing to do with how important an area is. Fraud prevention is not easier than financial advice — quite the reverse — but its error structure is friendlier. Choosing an area is not about difficulty; it's about how observable the errors are.

The only area stable at L2Payment risk. It is the reference case for the whole trend line — not because it's the hardest, but because it has all three prerequisites by nature.
"May change state, not move money"This is the real boundary of most controlled execution agents today. It is both a product boundary and a liability boundary.
Why research is stuckNot because the model answers badly, but because suitability and mis-selling rules require an accountable natural person. Until the rules change, the level won't.

1.4  Why it moves in steps, not gradually

If the trusted amount rose along a smooth curve, the industry would reshuffle gently: every company would climb at its own pace and first-mover advantage would be limited. But I don't think the curve is smooth. It hugs the floor for a long time, then jumps up in steps. There are three reasons, none of them technical.

First, liability is discrete. Law and contracts don't accept "the system is 70% responsible." Liability sits either with the human or with the system provider. So every step up must come with an explicit transfer of liability — and liability doesn't transfer gradually. It takes a specific date and a specific clause.

Second, insurance is discrete. No actuarial basis, no policy; no policy, and no institution dares hand over routine decisions. Actuaries need incident data, and incident data requires someone to have tried without insurance first. It's a classic cold-start problem: the market either stalls indefinitely or suddenly opens at a tipping point.

Third, audit is discrete. Auditors can't issue a "partially compliant" opinion. Either a system can fully reconstruct its decision chain or it can't. Full reconstruction needs an entire infrastructure — an event bus, tamper-proof logs, versioned models and prompts, permission snapshots. None of it is any use until it is finished, and all of it is usable the moment it is.

Finance has been through exactly this pattern before. The card industry's migration from magnetic stripe to chip used technology that had long been mature; the real migration came after the liability shift took effect — the rule that if a merchant hadn't upgraded its terminals, it bore the loss from counterfeit-card fraud. The technology was ready for years; behavior changed within a year or two either side of the rule.

I believe every step up for AI in finance will happen the same way: a long stretch of technical preparation and scattered pilots, then a regulatory or industry-level allocation of liability, then rapid, almost forced adoption. So what's worth watching isn't model launches but changes in the wording of liability clauses.

Three discontinuitiesLiability, insurance, audit. None takes continuous values, so stacked together they are bound to produce steps.
Insurance's cold-start loopNo incident data → no actuarial model → no policy → nobody dares use it → no incident data. Breaking the loop usually takes a big player willing to self-insure, or a regulatory push.
A historical parallel: the card liability shiftChip technology was mature for years, yet adoption clustered around the date the liability rule took effect. Rules changed behavior; technical availability did not.
What to watchNot benchmark scores, but how tender documents, insurance terms and regulatory Q&As word "who bears the loss." Changes in those texts lead market behavior.

2026 — 2027: the contract window. Once "ownership of evaluation sets," "audit retention periods" and "exit and data return" appear as standalone clauses in tenders, the governance stack has gone from nice-to-have to mandatory.

2.1  Three stages of expansion

Once a system has L2 working in one area, the team's next move is almost always the same: not to make that area better, but to connect it to the one next door. That's not ambition; it's economics. Automation gains in a single area plateau quickly, while connecting an adjacent process costs far less than acquiring customers from scratch.

Stage one: a single closed loop. Read, judge, act and audit within one area. The boundary is clear: the system touches one document type, one interface and one accountable owner. Its value ceiling is equally clear — roughly that area's labor cost plus its error cost.

Stage two: across processes. Chain adjacent steps together — reconciliation into collections, collections into provisioning, provisioning into filing. Value multiplies rather than adds, because what gets removed is the waiting and information loss between steps. But the hard part changes immediately: different processes usually have inconsistent permission systems, identity definitions and approval chains, all of which were invisible at stage one.

Stage three: across institutions. One institution's systems interact directly with another's — a supplier's receivables matched against a buyer's payables, a merchant's dispute against an issuer's rules. This stage holds the most value, but what it needs is no longer within any one company's control: protocols, liability allocation and dispute arbitration.

The stages aren't linear. Each step up adds roughly an order of magnitude of achievable value — and an order of magnitude of governance complexity. And governance complexity usually arrives before the value does. That's why so many teams stall at stage two: they underestimate the work of unifying permissions and identity, which is neither glamorous nor easy to charge for.

CHAPTER 2 · THE FINANCIAL OPERATING SYSTEM2.2 When machines become customers

2.2  When machines become customers

Cross-institution expansion raises a problem finance has never seriously dealt with: the party initiating a transaction may not be a person. Not a person using a tool, but an authorized software agent acting for an individual or a business, choosing counterparty, timing and amount on its own.

This isn't a distant hypothetical. Shared payment tokens, agentic commerce protocols, payments MCP servers and AI wallets are all early answers to the same question: how do you let a software agent safely spend someone else's money?[16][14][24] The technical details differ, but the problems they have to solve are the same.

Who gets verified changes. Traditional know-your-customer checks verify a natural or legal person. An agent is not a legal person; it is a chain of delegation: a user authorized a business, the business configured an agent, and the agent took an action at a given moment. Institutions need to verify the integrity and validity of the whole chain, not just the request at the end of it.

What fraud looks like changes. Fraud prevention used to spot "transactions that shouldn't happen" — stolen cards, counterfeits, account takeover. The hardest category in the agent era is the legitimate agent that oversteps: the authorization is real, the agent is real, but what it does goes beyond what the user actually intended. Transaction features alone can't catch this; it takes an explicit statement of authorization scope.

How bargaining works changes. People stay with an institution out of brand loyalty, habit and interface friction; machines don't. When an agent can compare ten providers' fees and settlement times in milliseconds, margins sustained by information asymmetry and switching costs get squeezed systematically. That's bad news for any business that lives on them.

Who owns a dispute changes. If an agent places the wrong order, whose fault is it — the user's, the business's, the agent vendor's, or the institution that accepted the transaction? Today's chargeback rules are built around "the cardholder didn't authorize it." They have no slot for "it was authorized, but the agent misunderstood." That is the central issue of Chapter 3d.

2.3  Settlement, identity and pricing rearranged

If machine customers become the norm, three pieces of infrastructure get rearranged first. What they share is that today's designs assume transactions are initiated by people, happen at limited frequency, and carry a meaningful amount each.

Settlement. Machine transactions tend to be smaller, more frequent and more fragmented. Faced with that flow, the fixed per-transaction cost of today's clearing systems becomes hard to bear — not because it's technically impossible, but because the fee structure doesn't fit. There are three likely responses: batch netting, book-transfer within accounts with periodic clearing, and execute-first-reconcile-later against pre-funded limits. All three lead to the same conclusion: whoever holds the balance captures a new economic benefit.

Identity. What's needed is no longer an identity but a verifiable, scoped, revocable delegation credential, able to express something like: "this agent may, over the next seven days, pay allowlisted counterparties for this category of goods, up to a cumulative amount." Whoever defines the standard for such credentials decides who sits at the center of the authorization chain.

Pricing. When the cost of comparison approaches zero, differences between products collapse onto a few quantifiable dimensions: fees, settlement time, success rate and dispute handling. The premium for brand and interface falls sharply. Good news for new entrants, bad news for institutions that charge on existing relationships.

Taken together, the three suggest where value will move: from the interface that touches the user, to the account that holds limits and credentials, and then to the protocol that defines what an authorization means. Interfaces get cheap, because agents don't need them. Accounts matter more, because limits have to live somewhere. Protocols become decisive, because they decide whose authorization is recognized.

2.4  Who becomes the operating system

"Operating system" is an overused term, so it needs a strict definition. Something is an operating system if and only if it meets two conditions: others build on top of it, and it defines the permission model. The second matters far more than the first — real control comes from deciding who may do what.

By that standard, no AI product in finance today is an operating system. But three kinds of players are closing in from different directions, and they are approaching different layers.

Payment networks are closing in on the execution layer. They already have money-movement interfaces, outcome data across merchants and working dispute processes. What they lack is command of facts inside the enterprise — they know how money flows, not how it should be booked.

System-of-record vendors are closing in on the fact layer. Tax and accounting software, terminals and data platforms hold the authoritative version of business facts. What they lack is an execution interface — they know what should be done but have to go through someone else to do it.

Super-apps and ecosystems are closing in on the entry layer. They combine use cases, identity and payments, and in theory are closest to full-stack. Their problem isn't capability but openness: a closed ecosystem can become its own operating system, but rarely the industry's.

My view: there will be no single AI operating system for finance, but a three-layer division of labor — payment rails handle execution, systems of record hold the facts, and a governance layer handles evidence and accountability. The layers will fight hard over their borders, but none will swallow the other two, because their strengths come from entirely different sources: one from licences and networks, one from customer data and switching costs, one from regulatory recognition and professional liability.

If this holds, the layer most worth watching is actually the third. The execution and fact layers have clear incumbents, while the governance layer is nearly empty today — nobody is systematically turning evaluation, logging, permissions and exit into products reusable across institutions. This book considers it the most underrated position.

CHAPTER 3A · FIGHTING FOR THE RAILS3a.1 Why execution rights don't dilute

3a.1  Why execution rights don't dilute

Over the past three years, almost every AI-related cost has fallen: inference per unit, fine-tuning, deployment, even the headcount needed to build a working product. That broad decline has created an illusion — that every moat will be eroded by technical progress.

Look closely, though, and only one kind of cost has fallen: the cost of judgment. Getting a system to know what to do has become cheap. Getting a system permitted to do it has not.

The cost of access is made of four things, none of which has anything to do with compute. Licences: initiating money movement usually requires authorization, and obtaining it takes years. Clearing networks: connecting requires counterparties to accept you, and acceptance depends on history, scale and trust. Dispute processes: handling things after they go wrong is a whole organizational capability, not an API. Liability capacity: you need a balance sheet or insurance to absorb the consequences.

The cost curve for all four is flat or even rising — tighter regulation makes licences dearer and liability requirements heavier. The result is a scissors gap: the cost of judgment is collapsing while the cost of access holds or rises. The two blades of that gap point in the direction value is moving.

This explains something that is otherwise hard to explain. Over the past two years, the companies with the strongest models have not won a matching commercial position in finance. Instead, companies with ordinary models but control of payment or ledger interfaces turned AI into revenue. The difference isn't who is smarter; it's who is allowed to act.

The value of the network shows up in the same place. Stripe says about 92% of transactions on its network come from a card it has seen before, with models trained on more than 70 trillion data points.[18] Data like that can't be bought — it is a by-product of execution rights running continuously. A company must first be allowed to act before it can accumulate the evidence that keeps it allowed to act.

That leads to a fairly brutal conclusion for founders: if your product's value comes entirely from the quality of its judgment, you are standing on the side where costs are collapsing. The next generation of models will erase today's edge, and you are accumulating no asset that resists technical deflation. The only way out is to attach judgment to some form of execution or liability as fast as possible — even if that means slower, heavier and less glamorous.

Break "access cost" down further and you get four control rights that can be held — and taken away — separately. The combination a company holds decides whether it collects rent along this value chain or merely takes orders.

Data rights are ownership of continuous outcome labels: data refreshed automatically with every transaction, which goes stale the moment the business stops. They differ fundamentally from purchased corpora — one is a stock, the other a flow.

Liability rights are the ability to present complete evidence and absorb losses after something goes wrong. They are often mistaken for a cost; in fact they are the pass into core processes — without them, the other three stay stuck at the advice layer.

3a.3  Where China stands

Apply the same framework to China and you get a clear but lopsided picture: very strong execution rights, a thin fact layer, and a governance layer under construction.

Execution is China's thickest layer. The depth of mobile-payment penetration, density of use cases and degree of identity binding are world-leading. The governance layer is being rapidly shaped by regulators. Put the three layers together and the strategic implications are quite concrete. Chinese companies already lead at the execution layer and have nothing left to prove there. Catching up at the fact layer offers poor returns, because it takes years to accumulate. The prize actually worth fighting for is the governance layer — regulators have only just defined it, it has no incumbent, and once it takes shape every cross-institution transaction will have to pass through it.

Once a system is allowed to move money, the shape of the attack surface changes. Traditional information security asks "could data leak?" The agent era must also ask "could permissions be misused?" A data-rights register belongs at project kickoff, not bolted on during legal review.

Two: access permissions. Agents often need broader permissions than people — reading several systems, calling several APIs. The most common mistake is making convenience the default: give the agent a high-privilege account and rely on the prompt to constrain it. A prompt is not a permission boundary. The real boundary must live at the API layer, in the token's scope, not in natural language.

Three: audit and keys. The decision chain must be fully reconstructable: what the inputs were, what was retrieved, which model and prompt versions ran, what the permission snapshot was, and what was finally executed. The integrity of that evidence itself needs protecting — if the system writing the logs can also alter them, they're worthless as evidence.

Together the three impose a direct requirement on product design: governance must live in the architecture, not in procedures. "Operating guidelines" written in policy documents are almost useless against agents, because agents don't read policy documents; they are constrained only by interfaces and tokens.

A misconfigured permission at one institution is a product defect, fixable with engineering discipline. The genuinely hard problem is a different one: the whole industry depending on the same handful of vendors.

This isn't a theoretical worry. This is fundamentally different from traditional model risk. If a bank's in-house credit model fails, the damage stays with that bank. If an entire industry's fraud systems sit on the same few foundation models, one shared failure mode can open holes at many institutions at once. Finance has learned similar lessons before, from credit rating agencies and from risk models.

This risk is currently priced at almost nothing. Procurement assesses a vendor's financial stability and security certifications, but rarely asks "what happens if this vendor also serves ten of my peers?" The question won't resolve itself any time soon, because it's never the top priority for any single buyer.

CHAPTER 3C · THE TRUSTWORTHINESS PROBLEM3c.1 Fix the structure, not the model

3c.1  Fix the structure, not the model

Faced with the fact that models can be wrong, engineering teams' first instinct is usually to make them wrong less often: better prompts, more fine-tuning, stronger retrieval. That path has value, but it has one fatal property — it never reaches zero. And what finance needs isn't zero errors; it's consequences that stay contained when errors happen.

That distinction produces two entirely different product philosophies. The first tries to make components reliable enough. The second accepts that components are unreliable and designs a structure that can contain them. Finance itself is a product of the second philosophy: double-entry bookkeeping assumes clerks make mistakes, four-eyes review assumes one person will err, limits assume judgment will fail, and clearing and reconciliation assume transmission will go wrong.

So the right question isn't "is this model accurate enough?" but "when it's wrong, who notices first, how big is the loss, and can it be reversed?" The answers to those three questions matter far more than two extra points of accuracy.

This brings a conclusion engineering teams may find uncomfortable: most of the work of trustworthiness is not on the model side. It's in limit design, trigger rules, rollback paths, event logging and the hand-off interface with humans. None of it makes a dazzling demo, but it is the only bridge between L1 and L2.

Intuit's approach is instructive here. It doesn't pretend AI can replace tax experts; instead it explicitly builds review by CPAs and tax experts into its products and services.[19][20] That looks like admitting the technology falls short. In fact it is using a structural component to buy entry into a high-liability arena.

CHAPTER 3C · THE TRUSTWORTHINESS PROBLEM3c.2 Five structural components

3c.2  Five structural components

Made concrete, the principle of the previous section yields five components of trustworthiness. They aren't options but a minimal complete set — without any one of them, a system can't safely cross into L2.

1. Limits. A per-transaction cap, a daily cumulative cap, a per-counterparty cap. Limits don't prevent errors; they put a tolerable upper bound on the total size of errors. They should be adjustable by customer, scenario and time, and every adjustment must be logged.

2. Circuit breakers. When anomaly rates, amount distributions or decline rates drift far enough from baseline, execution stops automatically and passes to a human. What matters isn't how precise the threshold is, but that it is triggered by the system itself rather than depending on someone happening to watch a dashboard.

3. Dual-track checks. Re-check critical decisions along a path independent of the main model — a rules engine, a traditional model, or a model from a different source. Independence matters more than accuracy: two paths from the same source will make the same mistake at the same time.

4. Human-review triggers. Write "when a human is needed" as decidable rules, not "confirm manually when it matters." Workable triggers include confidence below a threshold, amounts above a tier, customer segment, historical anomaly flags and a first transaction with a new counterparty.

5. Reversible execution. Prefer action forms that can be undone: pre-authorization rather than immediate capture, drafts rather than submissions, freezes rather than transfers. When an action is irreversible, human-review triggers must tighten significantly.

The five involve an important trade-off: tightening any one of them lowers the automation rate. So the core work of product design is finding the combination with the highest automation rate within a given risk budget — an optimization problem you can quantify, iterate on and show to customers. That is where the real technical depth of these products lies.

3c.3  Put it in the contract

While the five components exist only in code, they're engineering details. Once they appear in a contract, they become commercial assets. This is the step technical teams are least used to and the one that matters most.

The reason is simple: the buyer's procurement, risk and legal teams need something they can sign. "Our system is robust" can't be signed. "A per-transaction execution cap of ¥10,000; automatic circuit break when anomalies exceed twice the baseline; mandatory human review above ¥50,000; errors compensated up to this cap" can. Translating technical capability into clauses is, in essence, doing the customer's internal business case for them.

Writing clauses also improves the product: the moment you must commit to a compensation cap, you see which residual risks you won't carry — precisely where the product should go next. A contract is the most honest roadmap.

Six clauses need spelling out. They don't map one-to-one onto the five components in 3c.2; rather, they bind components, evidence and consequences together.

The most overlooked is the baseline and evaluation method. Without a human baseline both sides accept, any claim of "improved results" is just marketing; once the baseline is in the contract, outcome-based pricing becomes legally enforceable. It has to be done during the pilot; afterwards the manual process has changed and the baseline can't be measured retroactively.

Exit and data return is also a pricing tool: a vendor willing to commit to a full exit mechanism can charge a higher unit price, because the buyer saves an entire vendor-risk assessment.

3d.1  Three governance logics

Read the three jurisdictions' regulatory documents side by side and the direction is strikingly consistent: risk tiers, full lifecycle, traceability. But the logic that organizes those requirements is completely different — and the differences fall exactly where they affect product design most.

China organizes around the accountable party and the lifecycle. The NFRA's 32 guiding opinions of 2026 state that "whoever uses it is responsible," stress technological self-reliance, require coverage of the full chain from requirements, data and development through deployment, operation and exit, and focus supervision on high-risk scenarios.[10] The lever is the user: financial institutions bear full responsibility for the AI they use, and vendors are constrained indirectly through the institutions' compliance requirements.

The US organizes around use and materiality. Treasury's AI risk management framework for financial services and the revised model-risk guidance build on the existing risk-management regime, emphasizing tiering by use, materiality assessment, third-party risk, independent validation, transparency and resilience.[11] The lever is the materiality of the risk: the more material the model, the higher the bar for validation and governance — with materiality argued by the institution and examined by the regulator.

The EU organizes around risk classification. The AI Act places credit scoring and some insurance pricing among potential high-risk categories, requiring data quality, log retention, human oversight and robustness; DORA already covers ICT resilience and critical third-party risk.[12][13] The lever is the classification of the system itself: first determine what kind of AI it is, then apply the matching list of obligations.

The practical consequences are very concrete. Taking one product into all three markets means answering three different questions: in China, "can the institution using it prove control across the full lifecycle?"; in the US, "how material is this use, and is validation independent?"; in the EU, "which category does it fall into, and are the matching obligations all met?" The evidence for these three questions is not interchangeable.

That leads to a commercial conclusion: compliance adaptation will become a product layer in its own right. It isn't translating documents into three languages; it's mapping one set of capabilities onto three different evidence structures — the same logs organized and presented three ways. Companies willing to productize this can turn everyone else's cost into their own revenue.

3d.2  The standards gap in agent payments

All three frameworks discuss "how AI systems should be governed," yet almost none answers the concrete question raised in Chapter 2: when an agent initiates a transaction on a user's behalf and gets it wrong, who pays?

This isn't an oversight but a matter of timing — regulators usually step in only once a phenomenon reaches scale. But that gap is precisely the window in which standards form, and once formed, standards are extremely hard to change. Many of the rules in payments were settled in similar windows, and first movers' designs often became de facto standards.

Three kinds of players are currently trying to fill the gap, each with its own logic. Payment networks push shared tokens and agentic commerce protocols, aiming to fold agents into existing authorization and dispute systems while changing as few rules as possible. Platforms and ecosystems push their own agent-access schemes (payments MCP servers, AI wallets and the like), aiming to make it work inside their own ecosystem first and export it later.[14][16][24] Model and device makers want the agent layer to stay neutral, so they aren't tied to any single payment provider.

Their interests genuinely conflict. Payment networks want liability to follow existing rules, which favor them; platforms want liability kept inside their ecosystems, where they control it; model makers want liability to fall on the payment side, because they can't bear financial risk. No party can set the standard alone, so the likeliest outcome is a long period of competing standards, until an incident at scale forces regulators to step in.

For Chinese companies this gap carries a particular meaning: an execution advantage only turns into cross-border revenue in a world where standards are mutually recognized. If standards fragment by region, even the strongest domestic execution capability remains a domestic asset. That gives participation in international standards discussions — even just proposing technical designs others can cite — strategic value beyond any direct commercial return.

CHAPTER 4 · HOW REGULATION ARRIVES4.1 Not prohibition, but access

4.1  Not prohibition, but access

Financial regulators rarely ban a technology outright. Their more usual tool is to raise the bar for using it — require validation, records, capital, human review. In effect this resembles a partial ban, but the form is entirely different: the technology isn't illegal; using it just becomes much more expensive.

The distinction matters enormously to product teams. If you expect a ban, your response is to lobby and wait. If you expect access conditions, your response should be to turn the conditions into features ahead of time. The second is an engineering task you can start today.

The outline of this form is already visible. The US Treasury's FS AI RMF, published in February 2026, isn't legally binding, yet it breaks AI governance into 230 control objectives arranged under govern, map, measure and manage.[11] The real effect of such "soft law" isn't punishment; it defines what examiners will ask — and what examiners ask ends up on procurement checklists. The tiering, full lifecycle, traceability, third-party risk and human oversight that all three jurisdictions require will eventually become concrete checklist items.[10][11][12] Once they do, vendors that fall short aren't fined; they can't even bid.

That's what I mean by "a shake-out without a ban." Suppose that from some year on, major institutions' tenders uniformly require an independent evaluation report, tamper-proof audit events, a fine-grained permission model, a rehearsed fallback plan and enforceable exit and data-return clauses. Most of today's active AI vendors would be eliminated on that one page — not because their products are bad, but because none of this was ever on their roadmap.

Conversely, this is the clearest opportunity in the book. Access conditions can be known in advance: the regulatory documents are public, the direction is consistent, and all that's left is translating them into features and documentation. Teams willing to finish this before anyone asks will, at the moment of the shake-out, hold a lead others need two years to close.

4.2  Three possible institutional arrangements

Beyond access conditions, three heavier institutional arrangements may emerge. What they share is that each turns something that is an internal company matter today into industry-level public infrastructure.

First: industry-level evaluation and certification. Regulators, industry associations or authorized third parties maintain public evaluation sets, and vendors must be certified to enter certain scenarios. Other regulated industries have mature precedents. The benefit is lower validation costs for every buyer; the risk is that the evaluation set itself gets over-optimized and becomes a new exam to teach to.

Second: mandatory incident disclosure. Institutions must report AI-related production incidents, and de-identified failure modes are shared across the industry. This is the most likely source of the data actuaries need, so it also unlocks the insurance window discussed in Chapter 1. If I had to pick one institutional signal to watch, it would be this one.

Third: direct oversight of critical third parties. When some vendors' failure would hit many institutions at once, regulators may bypass the indirect route of "institutions answer for their vendors" and impose requirements on vendors directly. The EU's DORA has already built this kind of mechanism for ICT resilience.[13] For leading vendors it cuts both ways: compliance costs rise, but so do barriers to entry.

The likely sequence: access conditions first (already happening), mandatory disclosure second, and direct oversight last, applying only to a few critical vendors. Product teams need to prepare actively for the first two; the third concerns very few companies.

Two common misconceptions are worth separating out. The first treats regulation as pure cost — in fact access conditions are also a competitive barrier: they keep out rivals unwilling to do this work, work you'll have to do sooner or later anyway. The second treats regulation as paperwork you can fill in afterwards — but audit trails, permission models and exit mechanisms are architectural decisions, and the later you change them, the more it costs, exponentially.

One more judgment about timing is worth recording: institutional arrangements usually accelerate after the first incident at scale. That means the industry's actual pace may be set not by regulators acting proactively but by a single public incident. So making "what evidence must we produce within 48 hours of an incident?" a design input is more useful than predicting when regulatory documents will be published.

Where I might be wrong

This chapter assumes regulation keeps tightening. But regulators also feel competitive pressure: if a major economy concludes that strict governance is hurting its competitiveness, it may loosen, and other jurisdictions may follow. That would fundamentally change this book's conclusions — the premium on governance capability would vanish, and speed and scale would once again be the only variables.

CHAPTER 5 · SIX BETS5.2 If you're building products in 2026

5.2  If you're building products in 2026

Translated into action, the six bets mean different things depending on where you sit.

If you're founding a startup. Don't build a general-purpose financial assistant. Pick an area where errors are observable and reversible, finish all five components — limits, circuit breakers, dual tracks, review triggers and rollback — and then find three institutions willing to share your first incident with you. Your moat won't come from the model. It will come from the fact that those three institutions were willing to hand you the permissions.

If you run products at a large institution. Your scarcest resource isn't budget; it's an attributable baseline. Before any AI project, spend two months building a human baseline and a gold-standard evaluation set — without them, three years from now you still won't be able to say whether the investment was worth it. And treat governance capability as part of the product roadmap, not as compliance's afterthought.

If you work in investment or strategy. Use "which control rights does this company hold?" as your first screen and "will the next generation of models erase its edge?" as your second. Demand faster returns from pure judgment-layer companies, because they're standing on the side where costs are collapsing.

If you work in regulation or policy. The most valuable step, in this book's view, is to push for mandatory incident disclosure and the sharing of de-identified failure modes. It solves three problems at once: institutions learn from one another, insurance becomes priceable, and concentration risk becomes visible.

What all four have in common: none of them needs to wait for technology to advance another step. Every recommendation above can start today, and each one gets more expensive over time while its payoff jumps when liability shifts. It's the classic "cheaper if done early" structure — and most organizations will wait until they have no choice.

Closing

The whole argument of this book fits in one sentence: the next decade of fintech won't be decided by whose model is strongest, but by who is allowed to move money when nobody is watching.

That variable is close to zero today, so nobody models it and nobody prices it. But it won't stay at zero forever — because nothing needed to move it off zero is out of reach. Limits can be designed, circuit breakers built, audits recorded, liability priced and insurance underwritten. They just haven't been systematically finished yet.

Whoever finishes them first will be standing on the allowed side when the next liability shift comes. And when that moment arrives, the market will suddenly discover it has been pricing the wrong thing all along.

APPENDIXC Method, discount rules and limits

Appendix C  Method, discount rules and research limits

Method

The approach: first use regulatory documents, audited annual reports and international-organization reports to establish what has already happened; then build conditional projections on those facts, giving each projection observable triggers and falsification conditions. Claims that can't be falsified are, as far as possible, left out.

The book does not forecast model capability or rely on any assumption about model performance — it rests precisely on the premise that model capability keeps commoditizing. If that premise falls, the scissors-gap argument in Chapter 3a is the first to fail.

Data verification statement

Every public figure cited was checked against its primary source on 11 September 2026: original regulatory surveys and legislation, company financial statements and results announcements, and official product pages. Nine statements were corrected as a result before the first edition was finalized, three of them substantive errors; the corrections are preserved in the repository's commit history.

That doesn't mean the book is now error-free. Four caveats remain. First, company-reported figures cannot be independently verified; the book uses them to judge direction, never to support a conclusion. Second, figures on official product pages change over time; the text reflects them as of the access date. Third, the author is not a finance professional and may misread regulatory documents. Fourth, anything marked "research judgment" or appearing in a projection chapter is the author's inference, not a statement of fact.

If you find an error, please raise it in the repository; corrections will be recorded in the version history. Research that circulates with known errors is more dangerous than research that admits it may be wrong.

Why publish the list of errorsOnly when readers know what changed do they know what to re-check. A hidden correction leaves everyone who cited the old version still wrong.
Why state the number of errors"Nine corrections, three of them substantive" is more useful than a vague "the data may be inaccurate" — the first can be checked; the second is just a disclaimer.

APPENDIXC Research limits (continued)

Evidence-discount rules

Company-reported efficiency gains indicate direction only; unaudited revenue and user numbers never enter structural conclusions; media valuations and funding rumors are not used at all; the number of product launches doesn't count as commercial proof; features still in beta count toward product capability but not commercial validation; "number of institutions covered" does not equal paid penetration and is always flagged separately when cited.

Writing principleLeave out claims that can't be falsified. This is the main difference between this book and most industry forecasts.
The most fragile premiseThat model capability keeps commoditizing. If one company builds a durable lead in finance-specific models, the scissors-gap argument fails first.

Research limits

This book is not an industry directory. It makes no valuation judgments about private companies and does not constitute investment advice. The five companies discussed illustrate different types of industry control point; they are not the only representatives of their fields. In-house bank development, insurtech, regtech and foundation-model makers appear in the value-chain discussion but are not analyzed separately.

Every statement about the period after 2026 is a conditional projection. Readers should revise their judgments quarterly as new facts arrive, particularly the ten signals in Appendix A. If a projection's falsification condition appears first, the right response is to overturn it, not to explain it away.

Three known blind spots

1. Insurance is barely discussed. AI in insurance has its own structure (underwriting, claims, actuarial regulation) whose logic differs markedly from banking and payments. Including it would blunt the argument, but its absence is a real gap in coverage.

2. A gradual geopolitical environment is assumed. Major technology export controls or a decoupling of financial infrastructure would require the cross-border projections to be rewritten entirely.

3. The perspective is mainly institutional. Whether consumers are willing to let agents spend money on their behalf is treated as exogenous. This may be the biggest untested assumption in the book.
